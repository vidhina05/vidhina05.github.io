The project in short

The problem

People with chronic asthma judge their limits during activity by gut feel alone, and caregivers usually find out only once symptoms are visible.

What I did

6 stakeholder interviews, a 12-person survey and a 7-app competitive audit, then a breathing check-in, activity and inhaler logging, and a one-tap caregiver alert.

The result

Tested with 5 people using think-aloud, eye-tracking and SUS: an average SUS of 86.5, 12 issues, and two redesigns planned for the home and caregiver screens.

menu_bookSecondary research

Asthma is common. Managing it while active isn’t.

Asthma affected an estimated 363 million people in 2023 and caused 442,000 deaths.1 I started with secondary research, reviewing 25+ papers, medical articles and case studies, and mapped what I knew and needed to learn in a KWL chart. Three findings framed the project.

  1. Care is reactive

    Many patients reach for their reliever inhaler only once symptoms are bad, instead of following a preventive routine. The global asthma strategy treats relying on reliever-only treatment as a risk in itself.2

  2. Activity is a trigger

    Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction affects a large share of people with asthma,3 yet patients get no guidance on how much effort is safe on a given day.

  3. Tools look backwards

    A review of 38 popular asthma apps found data was entered by hand and no app could give just-in-time support during the day.4

record_voice_overPrimary research

Who I spoke to

Semi-structured interviews in person, and an online survey through Google Forms.

6 semi-structured interviews, a 12-person survey on Google Forms and 1 field observation.

  • Patients

    1 interview, 12 survey responses

    The patient I interviewed has lived with asthma for 15+ years. Survey respondents were aged 18 to 50+, a third of them 18–25.

  • Doctors

    2 interviews

    They only see what patients remember at appointments.

  • Caregivers

    2 interviews

    A mother and a hostel warden, who step in once symptoms appear.

  • Physiotherapist

    1 interview, plus 1 field observation

    Pacing and breathing guidance stays inside supervised sessions.

What the research told me

  1. 9 of 12

    Patients judge their limit by feel, and often too late

    9 of 12 decide when to stop from body signals or past experience, so they react after breathlessness starts instead of before. 5 of 12 only notice their limit once symptoms escalate.

  2. 2 of 2

    Caregivers step in late

    Help is reactive: both caregivers act once symptoms are visible, because nothing tells them sooner.

  3. 2 of 2

    Doctors can’t see daily activity

    Both doctors rely on what patients remember at appointments, because nothing records activity and symptoms in between.

  4. 7 of 12

    Uncertainty makes patients avoid activity

    Not knowing their limit makes exercise stressful, so many cut back on it. 7 of 12 rated their confidence during heavy activity 3 out of 5 or lower, and 7 of 12 feel anxious or helpless.

  5. 11 of 12

    They want guidance, not another tracker

    11 of 12 said a tool that works out how much activity is safe each day would be very or extremely useful. That shows the need, not the answer: what the tool should actually do came from the interviews and testing.

Finding the root cause

  1. Why do people with chronic asthma struggle to manage physical activity?

    They can’t tell what level of effort is safe for them.

  2. Why can’t they tell?

    Nothing gives them feedback on their breathing during activity.

  3. Why is there no feedback?

    Current asthma tools are reactive, and don’t monitor anything as it happens.

Root cause: there is no proactive support that guides safe activity in the moment.

Problem statement

Madhushree, a college student living with chronic asthma, needs a way to notice how her breathing is before and during activity, and to get help sooner, because she decides by gut feel alone and only notices her limit once breathlessness has already started.

How might we…

  • help people with chronic asthma notice changes in their breathing before and during activity?
  • let caregivers know sooner when breathing gets worse?
  • give doctors a clearer picture of a patient’s activity between visits?

Goal statement

Breathe Safe will let people with chronic asthma check their breathing status, log activity and inhaler use, and alert a caregiver in one tap. This will help them stay active with less guesswork and get support before symptoms escalate. We will measure effectiveness through task completion, the issues people hit, and SUS scores in usability testing.

What I kept, and what I left for later

I used SCAMPER to push past “another tracker”, then broke the app into 9 task flows, each with its screens and clicks estimated, and an information architecture built on Rosenfeld, Morville and Arango’s Information Architecture for the Web and Beyond.

infoHow the breathing status works, and its limits

Breathe Safe doesn’t diagnose or measure anything. The status on Home is her own check-in (Comfortable, Slight discomfort or Difficulty breathing), and the weekly chart shows how those check-ins have trended. The app helps her notice changes earlier and share them; it doesn’t decide for her.

  • Next version: a “Difficulty breathing” check-in brings up the Notify caregiver card straight away, with a reminder to follow her asthma action plan and call emergency services if symptoms are severe.
  • After that: link the check-in to the green, yellow and red zones of the asthma action plan her doctor already gives her, so “safe to go” comes from her doctor’s plan, not from the app.

All five scored above the industry average of 68, and an average of 86.5 sits in the top grade, A+.5 Five testers is a small sample, so read this as a strong early signal.

Think-aloud: 12 issues

No task failures were recorded. The 12 issues were about clarity, labels and layout, not about getting stuck.

  • 1 highEmergency access wasn’t on the home screen
  • 7 mediumCards looked tappable but weren’t; Share and Download were combined
  • 4 low“Call” instead of “Phone”; radio button placement

The most shared finding: 3 of 5 people expected Caregiver support under Profile, and 2 expected the health report there too. I haven’t moved them yet: emergency help also has to stay fast to reach, so the next round would test Profile against the Care tab before changing it.

What eye-tracking showed

The first look usually landed on the colourful weekly stability graph, not the breathing status above it. For a health app that should answer “am I okay right now?” at a glance, that was the wrong order.

Planned fix 1

Home: status first, help one tap away

Found through eye-tracking and think-aloud.

Next version: a Notify caregiver card right under the breathing status, and the weekly graph moved lower and toned down, so the first look lands on “am I okay?” and emergency help is on Home. This also answers the one high-severity issue.

Planned fix 2

Caregiver: say exactly what gets sent

Found through eye-tracking and think-aloud.

Next version: keep Notify as the dark primary card and Call as the lighter option, and replace the one-line location note with a short “What will be shared” list: breathing status, live location and time.

Testing was done on the final screens shown above, so these two changes are designed but not yet built.

Same 10 tasks, same three methods, this time with people who live with asthma. Each fix has a baseline from round one and a target, so the result is a yes or no, not an impression.

What I learned

Clarity, not task failure

People could finish the core tasks. What slowed them down were vague labels like “Currently Breathing” and cards that looked tappable but weren’t.

Combining methods catches more

Eye-tracking showed where attention went, think-aloud explained why, and SUS gave an overall score. No single method would have found both problems.

Next time

I would test a first version earlier, then build the two planned fixes and run a second round, to measure the improvement directly.
