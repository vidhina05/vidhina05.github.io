The project in short

Resham Riwayat, in short

The problem
Banarasi handloom weavers are left out of online markets, and buyers can’t easily tell a handwoven saree from a cheaper power-loom copy.
What I did
Interviewed a Varanasi workshop’s owner, manager and an artisan, documented the 21-step weaving process, compared 4 competing brands, then designed a desktop storefront.
The result
A high-fidelity storefront with an authenticity badge, a craft-first home page, a 5-step checkout and photographer booking, plus a “Who wove this” panel and weaver flow, designed as the next version.

A centuries-old craft, almost invisible online

Banarasi silk weaving is rooted in Varanasi’s cultural identity and carries generations of skill. This project set out to document the process from yarn to finished saree, understand the weaving community, and find sustainable ways to support artisans through visibility, fair value and voice.

Brocade weaving with gold and silver thread became Banaras’s speciality in the Mughal era, but power looms, which spread through the city in the 1980s and 1990s, have pushed most weavers off the handloom,6 and in 2023 a handwoven saree selling for about ₹10,000 could earn its weaver only ₹1,500–2,000.1

Our mission: to change how authentic Banarasi sarees are discovered and bought, by connecting buyers directly with the weavers behind every saree, so their craft is seen, valued and carried home with its true story intact.

In a Varanasi workshop

I interviewed the workshop’s owner, manager and an artisan, and documented all 21 steps from raw yarn to a packed saree. Photos are blurred to protect the people in them.

What they told me

  • Weaving is a family legacy and a source of pride. They welcome time-saving tools, but not anything that replaces traditional skill.
  • Marketing is their biggest challenge. They need direct reach to buyers, fair pricing, and something that proves authenticity.
  • Artisans learn by doing, so anything built for them should rely on visuals, voice and regional language, not text-heavy menus.

How it shaped the design: the craft comes before the catalogue. The home page opens with the history of the weave, and the product page carries an authenticity badge; weaver profiles come next.

Proto-persona, online buyer

Aaditi Sharma

“It’s so hard to tell if it’s handwoven or machine-made.”

Aaditi is an urban professional who loves Indian textiles. She buys Banarasi sarees for weddings and festivals, from exhibitions or boutiques she trusts. She values craft but rarely gets the story or a way to verify what she buys. She finds brands on social media and will engage online if it feels personal and real.

Goals

  • Buy an authentic, handwoven Banarasi saree
  • Support real artisans, not just retailers
  • Know the story behind what she wears

Pain points

  • Can’t tell handloom from power-loom
  • No way to verify where a saree comes from
  • Stores rarely share artisan information
  • Prices feel high and unexplained

Needs

  • Proof of handloom and quality
  • Photos, videos and the maker’s story
  • A simple purchase and delivery
  • Ways to connect with the craft

What would win her trust

  • Artisan profiles and process videos
  • GI tag or Handloom Mark details
  • Knowing her money reaches the weaver

Finding the root cause

  1. Why are weavers’ incomes falling while their work stays invisible?

    They rely on middlemen to sell their sarees.

  2. Why do they rely on middlemen?

    They have no direct access to buyers or markets.

  3. Why no direct access?

    They lack the digital skills, tools and platforms to reach customers themselves.

  4. Why are those missing?

    Training and support schemes rarely reach weaving communities.

Root cause: nothing connects weavers directly to buyers, or helps them build a presence online.

Problem statement

Aaditi, a 28-year-old professional who buys Banarasi sarees online for weddings and festivals, needs a way to know that a saree is genuinely handwoven and who made it, because power-loom copies are often sold as handloom and online stores rarely show the weaver or where a saree comes from.

At the same time, the weavers behind these sarees need a direct, fair route to buyers, because they lack digital skills, access and support, and depend on middlemen who keep most of the value.

How might we…

  • help online buyers trust that a Banarasi saree is authentic handloom?
  • make the weaver behind each saree visible to the buyer?
  • give weavers a direct way to reach buyers without middlemen?

Goal statement

Resham Riwayat will let saree buyers discover and buy authentic handwoven Banarasi sarees, see who wove them, and book styling or photography support.

This will help buyers trust what they buy and give weavers a fair, direct channel. We will measure it by whether buyers can find a saree’s weaver and origin, and by task success in usability testing.

Six clusters, nine concepts, four kept

I brainstormed ideas across authenticity, customisation, experiences and trust, grouped them into 6 clusters, combined those into 9 concepts, and kept the 4 that answered the problem statement directly.

Kept

Loom-origin stamp

A mark of where, and by whom, each saree was woven. It addresses trust directly.

Kept

Artisan stories

Raw materials, process and the weaver behind each saree, on the product page.

Kept

Buyers book a shoot in their saree, and the photos feed a customer gallery.

Kept

Choose a fabric and see how long a handwoven custom saree will take.

The storefront

Desktop first. A quiet ivory interface, so the sarees carry the colour and the craft sits inside the shopping journey.

“Who wove this”: the screen the project is about

Concept, not yet in the prototype

The prototype proves a saree is handloom with a badge. This panel goes further and puts the weaver on the product page. It is designed but not yet built, so it is the first thing the validation test checks.

  1. The weaver, by name. Answers Aaditi’s “Who made this?” and the workshop’s point that weaving is personal pride.
  2. A voice note, not a form. The workshop told me artisans work best with visuals, voice and their own language, so the weaver speaks and the site transcribes.
  3. Proof she can check. The Handloom Mark and GI tag already exist, but buyers rarely see them. Here they sit next to the price, and the QR on the tag brings her back after delivery.
  4. Where the money goes. No competitor explains its prices. A share bar makes a ₹50,000 saree easier to trust and shows the weaver is paid fairly.

How a weaver gets on the page

Concept, not yet in the prototype

The weaver side of the problem. Weavers rely on middlemen because they lack digital tools, so the workshop manager does the typing and the weaver only talks.

scienceValidation plan

How I would validate it

The project ended at the high-fidelity prototype. This is the test I would run next: can buyers trust a saree and find who made it?

Participants

5 people aged 25–35 who have bought, or plan to buy, a Banarasi saree online.

Method

Moderated think-aloud sessions on the Figma prototype, followed by a SUS questionnaire.

Tasks

  1. Find a festive saree under ₹40,000 using the filters.
  2. Find out who wove this saree, and where, using the “Who wove this” panel.
  3. Check whether it is genuine handloom.
  4. Buy it.
  5. Book a photographer for the wedding date.

Success measures

  • Task completion rate for each task
  • Time to find the weaver and the handloom mark
  • SUS score, aiming above the average of 68
  • Trust that the saree is authentic, on a 1–5 scale

The assumptions the test has to check first

The design rests on four things I haven’t proven yet. Each task in the plan is there to test one of them, and each has a clear answer for what I’d change if it’s wrong.

What I learned

A saree isn’t just a product

It carries generations of knowledge. The design had to show that, not just sell fabric.

The hardest problems are invisible

Low wages, digital gaps and market struggles are invisible to buyers. Making the weaver visible became the goal of the storefront, and it’s the part I’d push furthest in the next version.

Next time

Design the core screen first. The “Who wove this” panel is the one screen the whole problem points to, so it should come before checkout and booking. Then test with real buyers, and co-create the weaver flow with the artisans.
