The project in short

Problem

Vendors push 100–300 kg carts for 5–10 km a day over uneven roads, with no real steering, brakes or weather cover.

What we did

Interviewed 6 vendors on-site, used affinity mapping and a fishbone diagram to find the biggest problem, then sketched, modelled and priced a low-cost upgrade.

Result

A locally repairable upgrade with swivel caster wheels, a cycle handle with a brake, a canopy and organised crates, for an estimated ₹5,000–6,000.

A tool that hasn’t changed in decades

Heavy to push, hard to steer, no brakes, no weather cover. Our frugal-design question: what if the cart were redesigned around the person who pushes it, without pricing them out?

menu_bookSecondary research

What we knew before meeting a single vendor

Research papers, news reports, occupational-safety guidance and documentaries, reviewed to frame the problem before fieldwork. A KWL chart turned the gaps into research questions.

Long, hard days

A study of street vendors across seven Indian cities found most work 10 hours or more a day, and in several cities over half travel 10 km or more to reach where they sell.1 The vendors we met pushed 5–10 km and worked 10–12 hours.

Physical strain

Pushing a hand cart causes caught fingers, bumped feet, slips and falls, and strain to the back, shoulders and arms.2 A systematic review found strong evidence linking pushing and pulling to shoulder symptoms.3

Heat, rain, no shade

Most vendors work on open roadsides. In a 2024 survey of 721 Delhi street vendors, 49% lost income in heatwaves.4 Heat spoils produce; rain keeps customers away.

How today’s cart is built

  • Mild steel or iron frame, wooden plank top
  • Umbrella or cloth for shade and dust
  • Push handle, used to steer by force
  • Cart height set for pushing posture
  • Four large wheels for stability
  • Open top for display, partly organised
  • Weighing scale and tools kept on board

help_center5 Whys on the priority causes

Steering and stopping

  1. Why does pushing hurt so much? Vendors turn and stop a loaded cart with their body.
  2. Why with their body? The front wheels are fixed and there is no brake.
  3. Why are they fixed, with no brake? Carts are made locally to a cheap, basic design.
  4. Why that design? Better carts, like motorised ones, cost far more than vendors can pay.
  5. Root cause: there is no affordable way to add steering and braking to the cart a vendor already owns.

Storage and waste

  1. Why does produce get crushed? Crates are stacked on top of each other.
  2. Why stacked? The flat wooden top has no sections, so everything shares one surface.
  3. Why does waste sit in the cart? There is nowhere else to keep it during the day.
  4. Root cause: the cart was never designed to organise produce or waste.

low_priorityWhat we fix first

  1. SteeringNo steering control: vendors turn with their body weight, sometimes lifting the cart.
  2. StoppingNo braking system, which matters most on slopes.
  3. WheelsWheels not made for rough roads.
  4. Weather and storageHeat and rain, crushed produce and waste: important, but less harmful to the body.

We designed for the cart itself, the causes a vendor can change cheaply. Roads, distance and hours are out of scope.

Problem statement

Fruit and vegetable vendors like Ramesh, who push heavy carts 5–10 km a day, need an easier and safer way to move, steer and stop their cart, because poor steering, no brakes and heavy loads on uneven roads put constant strain on their bodies.

Based on our 6 vendor interviews and secondary research.

How might we

design an updated version of the traditional Indian fruit and vegetable cart that lets vendors travel longer distances with less physical strain, using materials that are affordable, durable and easily available locally?

Goal statement

The redesigned thela will let vendors steer, brake and protect their produce with cheap local parts. This will help them cover more distance with less strain. We will measure it with vendors on a working day: effort to push and turn, stopping on slopes, and total build cost.

Six design objectives

  1. Keep the cart’s traditional look and feel
  2. Make it easier to move and steer
  3. Let the vendor stop or slow down easily
  4. Protect vendor and produce from the weather
  5. Keep the produce clearly visible
  6. Give customers a better experience

All of it on a frugal budget, with parts sold locally.

Four things to prove on a real cart

Which wheels change

The two big spoked wheels stay at the rear and carry most of the load. Two swivel casters replace the front pair, so the cart turns from the front.

Caster load rating

A loaded cart can weigh up to 300 kg. If about half rests on the front, each caster carries around 75 kg. With a safety margin for potholes and kerbs, each needs a rating of at least 150 kg. We have to check that before buying.

Brake strength

A cycle brake is made for a rider and bike of roughly 100 kg. On a full cart on a slope it may not be enough. If it fades in testing, a drum or band brake on the rear axle is the fallback.

Added weight

The canopy, curtains, pipe frame and mesh add weight to a cart that is already too heavy. We’ll weigh the prototype against a standard thela; the casters and brake have to save more effort than the additions cost.

What I learned

The vendors set the order

The fishbone diagram traced most of the strain back to steering and stopping, so those became the first two fixes, ahead of shade and storage.

A budget is a design tool

Pricing every part on its own ruled out anything a local mechanic couldn’t fit or fix. It’s a big reason casters beat a steering linkage.

Shared research, split making

Nadiya and I did every interview together, then split the work: her sketches, my 3D model. It worked because we were both building from the same notes.

A concept is only a promise until a vendor pushes it

This is still a concept. Frugal design only proves itself against real cost and real vendors.

  1. Test with real vendors

    First, show the sketches and render to the six vendors we interviewed. Then build a working prototype and use it for a real vending day, not a lab walkthrough.

  2. Check the build cost

    Get real quotes from a local welder and compare with a standard thela.

  3. Watch the customers too

    Note whether the shade and evening light keep buyers at the stall longer, since that is what pays for the upgrade.
