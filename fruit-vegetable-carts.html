01 — Overview

A tool that hasn’t changed in decades.

Most fruit and vegetable vendors in Indian cities work off a hand-pushed wooden cart (thela) — heavy to push, with no weather cover, no secure storage, and no real place to sit or rest. This project asks a simple frugal-design question: what does the cart look like if it’s redesigned around the person who pushes it, without pricing them out of using it?

Project typeAcademic product design project
Target userStreet vendors selling fruit & vegetables from a pushcart
Design methodFrugal design — design for the base of the pyramid
Core constraintLow cost, locally repairable, buildable with existing materials & tools

02 — Secondary research

What we knew before meeting a single vendor.

Research papers, news reports and documentaries reviewed early in the project, used to frame the problem space before fieldwork with vendors began.

01

Travelling long distances

Vendors operate as mobile sellers between wholesale markets, residential areas and streets — typically within a ~5 km radius, 8–10 hours a day.

02

Mobility challenges

Manually pushed carts restrict how far a vendor can travel; crowded roads, pavements and markets make movement even harder.

03

Physical strain

Carts weigh 100–300 kg. Injuries include caught fingers, bumped feet, slips and falls, and strain to the lower back, shoulders, arms and joints.

04

Heat waves

Dehydration, headaches, fatigue and sunburn over 10–12 hr days with little shade — 49% lost income during heatwaves, and heat spoils produce.

05

Shade & sun exposure

Most vendors work on open roadsides without any infrastructure, which reduces customer footfall during peak heat.

06

Heavy rain & monsoon

Rainwater damages produce and discourages customers from stopping, directly affecting daily income.

Cart design analysis

How the existing hand-pushed cart is built

  1. Umbrella shade for heat protection
  2. Cloth covering for heat and dust protection
  3. Cart height set for a comfortable pushing posture
  4. Push handle for manual steering
  5. Storage space, partially organized produce
  6. 4 wheels for better stability
  7. Long-diameter wheels for load distribution and stability
  8. Weighing scale and tools kept on board

Modern hand-cart design study

Where newer cart designs are already heading

Waist handle height

Allows pushing without bending the back.

Swivel caster wheels

Two swivel + two fixed wheels: easier turning, better stability.

Large-diameter wheels

30–50 cm wheels reduce resistance on rough surfaces.

Angled display shelves

Customers see the produce easily from the street.

Bicycle addition

Reduces the physical effort to move the cart.

Canopy for sun protection

Built-in shade instead of an improvised umbrella.

Sectioned crates

Organized display that keeps produce separated and visible.

Handle grip

Natural grip that reduces fatigue and absorbs vibration.

Tyre treads

Softer treads grip better and absorb more vibration.

Steering mechanism

A linkage turns the front wheels — no dragging the whole cart.

03 — Primary research

We went to the vendors.

Six fruit & vegetable cart vendors were interviewed on-site through contextual inquiry, followed by affinity mapping to surface patterns across interviews.

Questionnaire — primary research

  1. What is your name and age?
  2. Location where you sell fruits/vegetables.
  3. Approximate distance travelled with the cart daily.
  4. Frustration points when moving the cart from one place to another?
  5. How do you store fruits and vegetables on your cart?
  6. Are some items stored or displayed at the bottom of the cart?
  7. Approximately how much produce waste happens daily?
  8. What do you usually do with spoiled fruits and vegetables?
  9. What challenges do you face during different weather conditions?
  10. Does your method of storing/selling change during different seasons?
  11. What is the biggest challenge you face while selling?

Affinity mapping

What vendors told us, in their own words

01

Physical strain from cart mobility

Pushing 5–10 km daily with a heavy load and poor wheels strains shoulders, legs, back and arms.

02

Challenges in steering & control

Narrow streets, no proper steering or brakes — vendors use body weight, sometimes lifting the cart to turn.

03

Exposure to weather

Constant heat, sun and rain cause fatigue and dehydration, and cut customer footfall.

04

Spoilage of produce

Heat accelerates spoilage; wet cloth or shade are the only — often insufficient — fixes.

05

Limited, unorganized storage

Produce gets piled together: crushing damage, poor airflow, harder for customers to see.

06

Waste management

Spoiled produce waits at the bottom of the cart or in plastic bags — no dedicated method.

07

Long working hours

10–12 hours a day, mostly standing or walking, from early morning to late evening.

08

Makeshift fixes already in use

Umbrellas, wet cloth, plastic sheets — informal solutions that work, but aren’t built into the cart.

05 — The problem

Street vendors manually push carts over long distances on uneven roads. Poor steering control, heavy loads and non-ergonomic cart design put real physical strain on their bodies — making mobility difficult and exhausting.

Problem statement · (PIB, Govt. of India, Dec 2025)

Design statement — how might we

How might we design an updated version of the traditional Indian fruit and vegetable cart that lets vendors travel longer distances with less physical strain — using materials that are affordable, durable and easily available locally?

(NASVI / Ministry of Labour)
0

shelter, storage, drainage, water or toilets guaranteed at most vending sites

(Centre for Urban Equity, IIT/CEPT, 2014)

What the research surfaced

Major issue

Mobility & maneuverability cause severe physical strain on the vendor’s body after pushing for long hours.

Minor issue

Storage on the cart could better keep produce organized and protected.

06 — Objectives of the design

Keep what works. Fix what doesn’t.

Six goals from the brief guided every decision — preserving what already works about the traditional cart while fixing what doesn’t.

01

Preserve the traditional look and feel

Keep the cart recognizable as a thela, not replace it with something unfamiliar.

02

Improve mobility and maneuverability

Make the cart easier to steer and push over long distances and uneven roads.

03

Easily stop or slow down the cart

Give the vendor real control on a slope or in traffic, not just momentum.

04

Protection against weather

Shield both produce and vendor from sun, dust and monsoon rain.

05

Ensure good visibility of the produce

Keep fruits and vegetables displayed clearly to the customer, day and night.

06

Good customer experience

The redesign should make buying from the cart feel easier too, not just selling.

08 — The final prototype

The same thela —
just kinder to push.

Modeled in SketchUp and rendered in 3D. Every addition answers a real gap the research surfaced, and each part was priced individually to keep the build affordable.

Solar bulb

Solar-powered LED for night-time selling without external power.

Organised crates

Modular arrangement for visibility, access and efficient use of space.

Storage system

Secured mesh compartment for empty containers.

Bicycle handle & brake

Repurposed handlebar with a brake lever for ergonomic control and effortless braking.

Canopy

Protection against sun, heat and rain.

Curtains

Adjustable covering that protects produce from sun, dust and rain.

Tent pipes

Lightweight framework that holds up the curtains and canopy.

Caster wheel

360° swivel for precise steering while supporting the front load.

09 — Reflection & next steps

A concept is only a promise until a vendor pushes it.

This stayed a concept-stage design project rather than a shipped product — which is exactly why the next steps matter. Frugal design only proves itself once it’s tested against real cost, real materials and real vendors.

Step 1

Test with real vendors

Build a working prototype and get it in the hands of vendors for a real vending day, not a lab walkthrough.

Step 2

Validate the build cost

Price the frame, canopy and bin with a local welder/carpenter against what a standard thela costs today.

Step 3

Pilot in one market

Run a small pilot in a single local market to see how the design holds up over weeks, not a single demo.
